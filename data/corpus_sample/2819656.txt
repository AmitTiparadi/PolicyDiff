May 25, 2022  12:07 IST  | Bennett University
  By Prof (Dr.) Nuzhat Parveen Khan
The advent of internet has changed the way humans live in a dramatic sense. In a world of Social Media, your personal information is not limited to the government records. The data available on the internet is overflowing with our personal information making it abundantly clear that we exist in a world of ‘Big Data’. The data we see relies on algorithms tracking our digital footprints. In the pre-Internet era people had the liberty to make mistakes, embarrass themselves and move on since people forgot about the experience soon. But in the present times, anything shared online, seems to exist forever.
In these times the right to privacy should also include the right to be forgotten. Right to be forgotten is the right to have personal information removed from publicly available sources, including the internet, search engines, databases or websites once the personal information in question is no longer necessary, or relevant. The year of 2020 was the year in which the world went more digital than ever before as a result of the pandemic that altered life as we knew it. One of the silver linings of the year was the spotlight on the importance of data and data flow. Taking this cue, the Indian Government took significant steps in tech policy and data regulation in 2020 such as non-personal data, health data, financial data, and data related to e-commerce and other consumer facing services. The judiciary has also made observations on individual rights regarding data privacy, and the ever-deliberated Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019 (PDP Bill) was a moving piece under government deliberation during the year.
The first case in India to deal with the concept of the right to be forgotten was the case of Dharmaraj Bhanushankar Dave v. State of Gujarat (2015 SCC OnLine Guj 2019, decided on 19-01-2017) in the Gujarat High Court. The Court held in the Petitioner’s favor and ordered that the name be redacted from the cause title and the body of the order. The Orissa High Court in the matter of Subhranshu Rout @ Gugul v. State of Odisha (BLAPL No.4592 OF 2020), emphasized the importance of ‘right to be forgotten’, a concept that has been upheld by courts abroad and recently encouraged by courts in India.
Section 18(1) of the PDP Bill provides that, “The data principal shall where necessary, having regard to the purposes for which personal data is being processed, subject to such conditions and in such manner as may be specified by regulations, have the right to…(d) the erasure of personal data which is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was processed.
Article 17 of The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) ((EU) 2016/679) which is a regulation in European Union law on data protection and privacy in the European Union (EU) and the European Economic Area (EEA) provides for the erasure of personal data on six grounds including no longer necessary.
In United States the decisions such as Melvin v. Reid (112 Cal.App. 285, 297 P. 91 (1931)) (1931) held, “any person living a life of rectitude has that right to happiness which includes a freedom from unnecessary attacks on his character, social standing or reputation” which gave birth to the right to be forgotten, although it’s still not codified by the legislature there.
The Supreme Court of India in the case of K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India ((2017) 10 SCC 1; AIR 2017 SC 4161), had held that the right to be let alone is an essential part of the autonomy and the privacy of an individual. The Supreme Court held, “If we were to recognize a similar right, it would only mean that an individual who is no longer desirous of his personal data to be processed or stored, should be able to remove it from the system where the personal data/information is no longer necessary, relevant, or is incorrect and serves no legitimate interest.” (Para 636)
There is a recent trend on protection of personal data generally and right to be forgotten particularly. The right to be forgotten is an integral facet of the right to life and personal liberty being part of right to privacy. This has been recognized nationally and internationally at various levels. But the right to be forgotten requires harmonization between the competing rights of right to privacy and the right to freedom of speech. The right to privacy in India, is a fundamental right implicitly and right to be forgotten could be a statutory right explicitly once the Bill of 2019 is enacted, although with certain desired modifications.
(The writer is Professor and Dean in School of Law)
